Open Letter to Donald J. Trump

What kind of world do you want to leave behind?

What kind of world do we want to leave to future generations?

Full open letter and supporting sources below. Personal views of the author; no affiliation with a political institution.

Mr. President,

I am writing as a French citizen and business owner, not to lecture the American people, but to ask a question that business leaders, families and citizens in many countries have every right to ask: what is the purpose of a nation's power if it no longer contributes to the stability and prosperity of those around it?

I do not confuse the United States, its people or its history with the decisions made by successive administrations. Your country has accomplished extraordinary things. It has also taken part in decisions with devastating consequences. Recognizing the former does not require us to forget the latter. And criticizing those decisions does not erase the courage of American soldiers or the generosity of millions of Americans.

This letter is an appeal to examine the whole record, assess the present, and consider the legacy you will leave in January 2029.

1. America did not build its greatness alone

Our shared history began long before the two world wars. On February 6, 1778, France and the American revolutionaries concluded a military alliance. Weapons, funding, troops and naval support: French assistance made a decisive contribution to American independence, particularly at Yorktown in 1781. The archives of your own country say so. [1]

With a touch of historical humor: without that help, Americans might still be drinking tea under the British Crown. The line may raise a smile, but the underlying fact deserves respect. The United States, too, has benefited from the solidarity of others.

In 1914, war devastated Europe long before the United States entered the conflict in April 1917. In 1939, another war began in Europe; the United States did not enter militarily until December 1941. American participation then proved decisive, and American service members made enormous sacrifices. It would be false to suggest that either war had already been won when they entered. It would be equally incomplete to forget that, before joining the fighting, Washington was pursuing its own security, supply and national interests. Allied orders also supported American industrial production and employment.

In 1940, fifty American destroyers were transferred to Britain in exchange for ninety-nine-year leases on British bases. Lend-Lease, enacted in 1941, provided vital support to the Allies while explicitly serving the defense of the United States. Some postwar financial arrangements involved repayment; that does not mean every shipment was sold or billed. American aid was genuine, essential and strategic. [2]

Then came September 11, 2001. European allies did not tell the United States to face the attacks alone. For the first time in NATO's history, Article 5 was invoked following an attack on American territory. France took part in the mission in Afghanistan; ninety French service members lost their lives there. That, too, is what an alliance means: Europeans took risks and died alongside Americans. [3] [4]

The transatlantic alliance is therefore neither Europe's perpetual debt to America nor an American act of charity. It is a history of reciprocity, shared responsibilities and, at times, shared sacrifice.

2. Yes, America has done a great deal of good. It deserves credit.

Over the past thirty years, the American PEPFAR program, launched in 2003, has helped save approximately twenty-six million lives in the fight against HIV. Other American efforts have supported vaccination, research, food assistance, emergency relief and disease prevention. These results are not slogans: they have changed the lives of entire families. [5]

I want to acknowledge that generosity, along with the efforts of American taxpayers, health professionals, researchers, charities, diplomats and volunteers. This is the America many Europeans admire: a country able to mobilize immense resources to save lives, share knowledge and create opportunities.

But it would be inconsistent to celebrate those achievements while accepting wars, trade rivalries and short-term decisions that undermine the very conditions that made them possible. Humanitarian aid must not become a permanent consolation for the damage caused by international policies.

3. Failures deserve the same candor

The interventions in Afghanistan and Iraq, counterterrorism operations and their aftermath have left a vast human and political toll. Brown University's Costs of War project estimates that, across several post-9/11 war zones, more than 940,000 people died directly from violence between 2001 and 2023, across all sides, while tens of millions were displaced. These deaths cannot simply be attributed to American forces alone; they illustrate the cost of conflicts in which Washington played a major role. [6]

After twenty years of intervention in Afghanistan, the Taliban's return in 2021 exposed the limits of trying to build a state through military force and reconstruction programs that were too often poorly designed or unsuited to local conditions. The American Special Inspector General for Afghanistan Reconstruction itself drew harsh lessons from the experience. [7]

A country that funds health programs while also contributing to destructive wars cannot ask the world to examine only one side of the ledger. It must also help repair, rebuild and prevent crises it has contributed to causing or worsening. Other belligerents bear responsibilities of their own.

There is no mathematical equivalence between a life saved and a life lost. There is, however, a moral obligation of consistency and responsibility.

4. Promises of peace must be judged by results

You promised to end the Russia-Ukraine war in twenty-four hours. In November 2026, the war that began with Russia's invasion of Ukraine is still not over. American diplomatic efforts are under way, and they deserve recognition; but they have not yet produced the result you promised. [8] [17]

In the Middle East, the first phase of your Gaza initiative led to a ceasefire in October 2025 and to the release of hostages and Palestinian prisoners and detainees. It would be unfair to overlook that achievement. Yet a year later, reconstruction, security and a political settlement remain largely unfinished, while violence continues. [9]

Regarding Iran, U.S. and Israeli strikes launched in February 2026 opened a regional conflict with grave consequences. Ceasefires and negotiations followed, but a durable settlement was still out of reach in early November. Energy markets and ordinary people have paid the price. The stated objective of security must be measured against actual outcomes. [10]

I do not pretend that any president can end every war with a single decision. But promising rapid peace creates a duty to be truthful: when wars continue, leaders must say so, explain why and reconsider methods that have not worked.

5. A policy of pressure is not a plan for prosperity

Tariffs wielded as punishments, public pressure on allies, threats of retaliation, uncertainty surrounding agreements: to me, this approach too often resembles a diplomacy of economic coercion.

Is that really the vision of global trade you wish to defend? Nations forced to yield for fear of losing access to the American market? Partners negotiating under threat rather than in trust?

I sometimes summarize that perception with a deliberately severe phrase: "strong against the weak, but weak against the strong." I offer it as a challenge to your method, not as a judgment on the American people. Is power most readily displayed against the partners most dependent on it? And what does it truly achieve when confronted with states capable of resisting?

In February 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court held that IEEPA did not confer the emergency tariff power you claimed. Checks and balances do exist, and they must be respected. A credible trade policy requires stable rules, democratic oversight and predictability. [11]

The European Commission finds that recent tariffs have so far caused limited direct damage to European exports, but have increased volatility and uncertainty, which weigh on investment. That is already a serious concern for any business deciding whether to hire or expand. [12]

6. The cost of conflict reaches the family shopping basket

Wars do not remain confined to military maps. Their effects are felt in the prices of fuel, gas, fertilizers, transport, raw materials and ultimately food. When energy prices rise, a business pays more to make deliveries; a family pays more to travel; and a farmer faces higher production costs. The shock moves through the economy.

Neither inflation nor European demonstrations have a single American cause: Russia's war against Ukraine, choices made by European governments, energy crises and structural factors also matter. But Washington cannot ignore the global effects of its own decisions. The Middle East conflict in 2026 demonstrated how quickly military escalation can disrupt energy prices. [10]

In October 2026, a Pew Research Center survey found that 65% of U.S. adults said your economic policies had made economic conditions worse; 74% were very concerned about energy prices and 73% about the price of food and consumer goods. These are measured perceptions, not proof that every American has become poorer. But they constitute a political and social warning. [13]

What is the value of proclaiming national prosperity when so many citizens, in America and Europe alike, worry about the next bill?

7. Without a prosperous Europe, American prosperity is less secure

Europe is not an adversary to be impoverished. It is a major economic partner. In 2025, trade in goods and services between the European Union and the United States reached approximately €1.77 trillion. Cross-border investment and European businesses operating on American soil support millions of jobs. American businesses need customers with purchasing power and reliable partners. [14]

If Europeans lose purchasing power, if companies postpone investment, and if every disagreement triggers a new tariff threat, the United States cannot imagine that it will remain insulated forever. Prosperity is not a pie made larger by starving one's neighbors.

The dollar remains the world's leading reserve and transaction currency. Yet its international role does not rest solely on the U.S. military: it also depends on the size of the economy, the depth of financial markets, the rule of law and trust in institutions, as the Federal Reserve explains. A country may possess the most powerful armed forces on earth and still lose part of the commercial and financial confidence on which its strength depends. [15]

Military strength can deter. It cannot command trust.

8. When private interests intersect with public office, integrity matters

I do not wish to ascribe motives that no court or investigation has established. Yet several investigations, including reports by Reuters, have documented the scale of income and profits connected to your family's cryptocurrency ventures. A June 2026 investigation estimated at least $2.3 billion in family profits across several projects, while the net losses of investors involved were estimated at a similar amount, including unrealized losses. [16]

Those figures do not prove a crime or establish any link between your family's wealth and a decision to go to war. They do, however, raise legitimate questions about conflicts of interest and the appearance of impartiality. When a head of state asks others to make sacrifices, he should be able to demonstrate that his choices serve the public interest, not private interests.

The success of one family cannot substitute for the success of policies affecting millions of families.

9. Washington, Moscow and Beijing do not own the world

Dialogue among Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping is essential when it reduces nuclear risks or prevents war. But I reject the idea that a handful of leaders should decide other nations' futures on their own. I am not suggesting that a secret agreement to divide the world has been established: I am challenging the historic temptation of great powers to treat other countries as spheres of influence.

Neither Washington, Moscow nor Beijing owns the world. Nations have sovereignty; people have dignity, interests and rights. Social media makes decisions more visible and facilitates mobilization, although it also spreads misinformation. Governing today without acknowledging people's desire to be heard is a mistake.

This criticism can also be addressed to Mr. Putin, who bears responsibility for the invasion of Ukraine, and to Mr. Xi Jinping when power politics prevail over cooperation. Their political systems and actions are not identical; the shared principle is that peoples must not be treated as instruments.

A strategy of "divide and rule" destroys trust. It encourages countries to seek refuge in protectionism, isolation, rival blocs and sometimes political radicalization. In Europe, populist movements on both the left and the right can benefit from social anger; that does not mean every protest has the same cause or that voters are incapable of making informed choices.

And when social and diplomatic tensions involve nuclear powers, including France, stability becomes a matter of international security, not merely political messaging.

10. When crises accumulate, international trust erodes

To understand the present world, we must also look at American interventions in historical perspective. From the overthrow of Iran's government in 1953 to Guatemala in 1954, from covert operations in Chile to the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, history shows that U.S. administrations have sometimes prioritized immediate strategic objectives whose consequences extended far beyond the countries concerned. These episodes were not one continuous policy, and the responsibilities of all participants must be distinguished. Yet they raise a question: years later, who bears the human and political cost of destabilization?

Your presidency includes several episodes that deserve the same scrutiny. In January 2026, U.S. forces struck Venezuela and captured Nicolás Maduro. Venezuela's government could legitimately be criticized for abuses of rights and its record in office. That does not remove the need to question sovereignty, international law and the consequences of a military intervention ordered from Washington [20]. Announcing a transition is not the same thing as demonstrating that stability has been restored.

In Ukraine, the initial aggression was Russia's invasion. Yet the public confrontation on February 28, 2025, involving you, your vice president and Volodymyr Zelensky in the Oval Office gave the impression of a leader whose country was under attack being publicly challenged in front of cameras. Effective diplomacy may demand painful compromises; it must also consider how its methods affect the confidence of allies and the signal they send to Moscow [21].

The Greenland dispute provided another warning. In January 2026, the prospect of tariffs against European allies to support your claims involving territory belonging to the Kingdom of Denmark unsettled NATO. You subsequently withdrew the threat and described a possible framework for an agreement. That reversal matters, but it does not erase the uncertainty caused by the earlier approach. An ally should not have to wonder whether its territorial integrity could become the subject of negotiations under pressure [22].

The U.S. Department of Defense's acceptance of a Boeing 747 offered by Qatar in May 2025 raised security and ethical questions. Acceptance by a government institution is not proof of a personal gift to you, but it still raises questions about the appearance of dependence on a foreign state [23]. Similarly, a draft charter for the Gaza Board of Peace, reviewed by Reuters in January 2026, provided for a contribution of one billion dollars to escape the ordinary three-year membership limit. It was not a mandatory fee simply to join. Why should a lasting seat depend on a country's ability to commit such a sum [24]?

Finally, immigration policy cannot be reduced to a demonstration of toughness. Border controls, asylum procedures and the rule of law are legitimate responsibilities of governments. Yet immigration enforcement actions in 2026 disrupted meat-processing operations in Kansas, according to Reuters. A country can seek orderly immigration without treating the people who sustain its farms, services, research and economy as expendable [25].

Each of these issues requires its own analysis. Together, they pose a question of governing style: should predictability, law and trust give way to the permanent use of pressure? The United States has not caused every crisis in the world. But it cannot disregard the global consequences of its own choices.

11. When multinational corporations become political powers

Another transformation threatens democratic balance: some companies have accumulated such financial, technological, logistical or informational power that they can exert substantial pressure on sovereign governments. Corporate turnover cannot properly be compared with a country's gross domestic product, and a company is not a state with legislative and judicial powers. But a multinational able to condition jobs, infrastructure, access to data or market access possesses considerable political leverage.

Guatemala provides a historical warning. In the 1950s, United Fruit Company held extensive land and a dominant position in the country's transportation and exports. When the land reform of elected President Jacobo Árbenz threatened those interests, commercial concerns became intertwined with Washington's anti-communist objectives. American archives establish that the CIA organized the operation that helped overthrow Árbenz in 1954 [18]. It would be wrong to say the company alone decided the coup; it would be equally wrong to erase its interests from the context.

In Chile in 1970, the American multinational ITT had documented contacts with the CIA in the context of Salvador Allende's election. An offer of one million dollars to the agency was refused; other contacts and funding channels supporting the opposition were examined by the U.S. Senate. These facts do not prove that ITT alone caused the 1973 coup. They do show how private interests can seek to influence a country's political future [19].

Today, influence also takes less visible forms. Some platforms control access to information, customers, advertising and digital services that have become essential. In its 2026 report on the Digital Markets Act, the European Commission observed that a small number of gatekeepers dominate extensive digital ecosystems [27]. The OECD likewise finds that lobbying rules and the transparency of those who ultimately benefit from lobbying remain inadequate in many countries [26].

I am not opposed to business. I am an entrepreneur. I know businesses create jobs, take risks, innovate, invest and contribute to collective prosperity. But economic success must never become a license to influence an election in secret, secure a military intervention, or write rules that only competitors and employees are expected to obey.

The answer is not to replace the market economy with arbitrary state control. It is to make lobbying contacts and conflicts of interest transparent, strengthen oversight of political financing, prevent self-serving revolving doors between government and industry, enforce fair competition, protect critical infrastructure and ensure equitable taxation. The economy must serve democracy; it must never become its owner.

12. Governing for people, not merely in their name

The problem goes beyond the United States. In France and elsewhere, a lawful election grants a legal mandate, but not a moral blank cheque for an entire term. When distrust of a president, government or parliament becomes deep, merely pointing out that the constitution allows officials to remain in office is not enough. Listening, explaining, correcting course and accounting for decisions matter as well.

This question also concerns President Emmanuel Macron and, more broadly, every French political movement. It does not mean that a low poll rating annuls a constitutional mandate or that a new presidential election can simply be ordered at will. It means that democracy loses vitality when citizens feel they are being governed despite their wishes rather than with their involvement.

Citizens contribute through taxes, support public services and sometimes accept painful collective sacrifices. In return, they have a right to verifiable integrity from officials and those close to power: lawful declarations of interests and assets, independent oversight, prevention of favoritism, transparent public expenditure and effective sanctions for wrongdoing. It would be irresponsible to claim that every politician is corrupt or repeat unverified estimates of personal fortunes. It would be equally irresponsible to deny the dangers of private capture of public decisions.

The question is therefore not which leader one likes. It is more demanding: what real power do citizens retain between elections, and who actually benefits from public decisions?

13. Another democracy is imaginable: continuous citizen oversight

I would like to put forward an idea that may sound utopian, although elements of it already exist in different democracies: a republic in which citizens do not merely choose their representatives but retain regular tools to scrutinize public action throughout each term.

A popular initiative could require lawmakers to consider an important bill or decision once a reasonable, verifiable signature threshold had been met. A citizens' assembly selected by lot, broadly reflecting the country's social diversity, would hear opposing evidence, question experts, unions, businesses and associations, and publish its conclusions. Parliament would be required to respond publicly and explain its decision; some questions could go to referendum, with fundamental rights protected.

An independent public portal would make state contracts, major expenditure, stated objectives, measurable results, lobbying meetings and conflicts of interest understandable, subject to legitimate confidentiality and security requirements. A defined share of public spending could be shaped by citizen deliberation, especially on infrastructure, education, health and energy. Carefully safeguarded recall or accountability mechanisms could be debated to strengthen responsibility without trapping the country in a permanent election campaign.

Such tools would not replace an independent judiciary, constitutional guarantees or minority protections. Even a directly consulted majority must not be able to strip rights from those who disagree. Technology could support consultation and transparency, but decisions should never be delegated to algorithms or to manipulation through social media.

The OECD already documents hundreds of citizens' assemblies and other deliberative processes [28]. Europe has begun regulating major digital gatekeepers. We would therefore not be starting from nothing; we would be connecting existing experiments into a new democratic requirement: power is delegated to serve; it is never surrendered without oversight.

Perhaps this is the real path to shared prosperity: neither governments against companies, nor companies against governments, nor citizens against their representatives, but institutions capable of aligning wealth creation, economic liberty, environmental responsibility and the public good.

14. It is time to measure greatness differently

I am not demanding that the United States pay every cost in the world. I am asking a great power to recognize its obligations toward the world of which it is part. That means pursuing verifiable and lasting peace agreements; protecting civilians; taking reconstruction seriously after wars; abandoning permanent trade threats; preserving effective humanitarian programs; cooperating on energy, food, health and innovation; and respecting institutions and commitments made to allies.

This does not mean abandoning American interests. It means recognizing that their long-term success depends on a stable Europe, a prosperous Asia, a developing Africa, open commerce and trustworthy institutions. It means investing in people rather than in an endless succession of crises.

Mr. President, what world will you leave on January 20, 2029?

This brings me to the essential question. What is the purpose of these policies? What are you trying to build? A nation admired because it unites, heals, trades and innovates? Or a nation that its partners have learned to fear, bypass and replace whenever possible?

What will remain of the promises of peace? What will remain of trust across the Atlantic? What will remain, for American families themselves, of the hope of a better life? A tariff victory or a triumphant speech cannot answer those questions.

You still have an opportunity to use American power in the service of shared prosperity. France, Europe and many other peoples are not asking for America's decline. They are asking for a relationship in which respect replaces intimidation and cooperation replaces threats.

And to Mr. Putin and Mr. Xi Jinping, I put the same fundamental question: what world do you intend to leave to the people who will live with the consequences of your choices? For dividing in order to rule is not building peace, and controlling is not the same as creating prosperity.

Mr. President, history will remember measurable consequences more than proclaimed intentions. It will recognize the lives your country has saved. It will not forget what decisions made by powerful leaders have helped destroy.

True greatness is not being the only one to win. It is creating a world in which others can succeed as well.

That, Mr. President, is the question I put to you frankly and respectfully: what will you actually have improved for the world's peoples when you leave the White House?

Respectfully yours,

Emmanuel Edel

French Entrepreneur

France
